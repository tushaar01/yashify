Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 2026

For discovering light-gated ion channels and developing optogenetics. The prize is shared equally by three laureates.

Karl Deisseroth

Psychiatrist and bioengineer, USA. Stanford University

Deisseroth is a professor of bioengineering and of psychiatry and behavioral sciences at Stanford. As a medical student he considered becoming a neurosurgeon, but his training at a psychiatric clinic pulled him toward understanding diseases of the brain.

His lab took the algae's light-sensitive protein into neurons and showed that specific brain cells can be controlled with pulses of light.

Nobel topic: Using channelrhodopsin in neurons, which became optogenetics.

Original research paper

  • Millisecond-timescale, genetically targeted optical control of neural activity Boyden et al., Nature Neuroscience, 2005

Peter Hegemann

Biophysicist, Germany. Humboldt University of Berlin

Hegemann studied the eyespot of green algae, the small structure that lets them sense light. His lab identified channelrhodopsin-1, the first direct evidence of an ion channel that opens in response to light.

Nobel topic: Discovery of light-gated ion channels (channelrhodopsins) in algae.

Original research paper

  • Channelrhodopsin-1: a light-gated proton channel in green algae Nagel et al., Science, 2002

Georg Nagel

Neurophysiologist, Germany. University of Würzburg

Nagel is a professor of neurophysiology at the University of Würzburg. Working with Hegemann, he showed that channelrhodopsin-2 lets positive ions flow into a cell the moment light hits it. That property made it the key tool of optogenetics.

Nobel topic: Channelrhodopsin-2, a channel that is opened directly by light.

Original research paper

  • Channelrhodopsin-2, a directly light-gated cation-selective membrane channel Nagel et al., PNAS, 2003

Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2026

For discovering non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis. The prize is shared equally by two laureates.

Henri B. Kagan

Chemist, France. Université Paris-Sud, Orsay

Some molecules come in two forms that are mirror images of each other, like a left and a right hand. Medicines usually need exactly the right "hand", and why nature uses only one hand of its molecules has been a puzzle for over a century.

Kagan is 95 years old. In 1986 he showed that the purity of a catalyst and the purity of the product it makes do not always follow a straight line. A partly pure catalyst can sometimes give a very pure product. This is called a non-linear effect, and it is now an important part of how chemists design reactions for making pharmaceuticals.

Nobel topic: Non-linear effects in asymmetric synthesis.

Original research paper

  • Nonlinear effects in asymmetric synthesis. Examples in asymmetric oxidations and aldolization reactions Puchot et al., with Kagan, Journal of the American Chemical Society, 1986

Kenso Soai

Chemist, Japan. Tokyo University of Science

Soai discovered a reaction in which the product acts as its own catalyst and keeps producing more molecules of the same "handedness". Even a tiny early imbalance grows into a large one. The reaction is now known as the Soai reaction, and it shows one way a single handedness could take over in nature.

Nobel topic: Asymmetric autocatalysis and the amplification of chirality.

Original research paper

  • Asymmetric autocatalysis and amplification of enantiomeric excess of a chiral molecule Soai et al., Nature, 1995

Nobel Prize in Physics 2026

For decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos from space. The whole prize goes to one laureate.

Francis Halzen

Particle physicist, Belgium and USA. University of Wisconsin–Madison

Neutrinos are extremely light, ghost-like particles that pass straight through matter, which makes them very hard to catch. Halzen led the effort to build IceCube, a giant detector buried in the ice of Antarctica.

IceCube caught high-energy neutrinos arriving from far out in the universe. This opened a new kind of astronomy: looking at the cosmos with neutrinos, not only with light.

Nobel topic: The IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin.

Original research papers

  • Evidence for High-Energy Extraterrestrial Neutrinos at the IceCube Detector IceCube Collaboration, Science, 2013
  • Multimessenger observations of a flaring blazar coincident with high-energy neutrino IceCube-170922A IceCube Collaboration and partners, Science, 2018
